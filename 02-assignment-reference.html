Running KlimaShift Smarter

Shekhar Suman · IIT Patna (B.Tech + MBA, OSCM) · Thinking assignment for Ankit Chouhan
My core idea in one line: KlimaShift sells numbers people can trust. Most errors are born upstream (at the panel, during install and mapping) but found downstream (in the savings report, often by the customer). So the first thing to automate is not "more AI insights". It is catching errors at the panel, before they reach a report.

What I don't know: I haven't seen your data, tools or team. Every number below is either from public sources, a stated assumption, or a starting target that I would reset after the first week of measurement. The questions I'd ask you are at the end.

2. The 5 things most worth automating

Rule I used: automate the checking and the routine. Keep humans on judgement calls and anything a customer will read. AI (LLMs) only where the task is language or images. Detection stays rules + site-specific statistics. Public benchmarks back this: in TSB-AD (NeurIPS 2024), simple statistical detectors often matched or beat deep models.

A. Auto-commissioning: a "site acceptance test" before the electrician leaves

What it does: when a kit powers up, the platform runs automatic checks on every channel. Is data arriving? Is power negative (clamp reversed)? Is the power factor impossible for that equipment, e.g. a compressor reading below 0.3 (clamp on the wrong phase)? Is current outside the nameplate range? Is the meter's supply voltage unstable? Then a switch test: the ops app asks the electrician to switch equipment X off and on, and confirms the right channel dropped. Equipment that can't be switched (a walk-in freezer) is verified from its compressor cycling over the next 24 hours.
Data: live channel readings, the planned channel map (from the survey), equipment type and nameplate, and switch-test timestamps from the ops app (which already does QR onboarding).
Stays human: fixing the fault; overriding a check (with a reason + photo).
If wrong: a false pass lets bad data through, which B catches later. A false fail wastes an electrician's night, so overrides are allowed and every override is reviewed.

B. Data-health monitor: a morning list of broken channels, ranked

What it does: every morning, checks every site and channel for gaps (>5% missing), flatlines on equipment that should cycle, sudden step changes with no logged action, device reboots or supply dips, stuck temperature sensors, and (monthly) meter kWh vs utility-bill kWh. Opens a ticket with the likely cause and whether a remote fix is possible.
Data: raw time series, device logs, action log, bills (parsed with Docling or an LLM from PDF or photo).
Stays human: deciding on a truck roll.
If wrong: a miss means a report built on bad data, so B also acts as the gate for D. A false alarm means a wasted visit, which is why truck rolls stay a human decision.

C. Alert triage and closing the loop

What it does: turns detections into a few alerts people act on. Equipment on after closing (vs store hours). Freezer short-cycling (starts/hour above 2× that unit's own 30-day normal). Power factor below 0.9 where billing is in kVAh, e.g. Maharashtra moved commercial users above 20 kW to kVAh in 2025. Temperature excursions. It de-duplicates, ranks by ₹ or risk, sends each alert to the right person on WhatsApp with one-tap Done / Not useful, then checks the energy data afterwards to see whether the action worked. An LLM only rewrites the alert in plain Hindi or English.
Data: detections, store hours, tariff, contact roles, feedback taps.
Stays human: anything that looks like an equipment failure, and any customer escalation.
If wrong: too many false alerts and customers stop reading all alerts (the biggest risk). Missing a cold-storage temperature excursion is a food-safety issue, so that class always sends and is never throttled.

D. Savings report: auto-drafted, human-signed

What it does: data passes B's gates → baseline model (energy vs outdoor temperature, store hours, day of week; open-source OpenDSM, formerly OpenEEmeter) → avoided kWh and ₹ as a range, not a single number → change-point detection (ruptures) flags unexplained jumps for the analyst to label → an LLM drafts the narrative. The LLM never writes a number: code inserts every figure, and a check confirms every number in the text matches the table.
Data: site energy, hourly temperature (Open-Meteo), store hours and closures, action log, tariff, optionally daily sales or footfall.
Stays human: labelling non-routine events and signing every report.
If wrong: an over-claim the customer discovers loses the renewal. An under-claim leaves value unproven. Both are covered by the eval in section 3.

E. Survey-to-kit: the audit produces the kit list and the channel map

What it does: the audit (panel photo + equipment list in the ops app) generates the bill of materials (clamp sizes and count, sensors), the pick list, and the planned channel map, which then becomes A's checklist on install night.
Data: survey form, equipment→clamp rules, stock sheet.
Stays human: reviewing the survey; unusual panels.
If wrong: a missing part means a repeat night visit. Easy to measure, easy to fix.

Two I would NOT automate yet

  1. ML failure prediction as its own product. It's the most tempting one, but there is no labelled failure history yet. Failures are rare per site, and a wrong "your freezer will fail" costs trust fast. For now I'd keep rules-based "watch" signals inside C (rising duty cycle, short-cycling) and start collecting labels: every ticket closed with "what actually broke". In 6–12 months that becomes the training and eval set.
  2. Closed-loop control (the platform switching equipment off by itself). It saves more in theory, but one wrong command on a freezer or a kitchen during service is a food-safety and brand problem for the customer. Schedules stay human-approved until A and B prove the data feeding them is right.

Deep dive: the savings-report eval (D), detailed enough to build

The real difficulty isn't the model, it's that there is no clean "before". The meter goes in on day 1, so there's no hourly history from before KlimaShift. I'd use three sources of "before", in this order. (1) A 2–4 week observation period after install with no actions taken. (2) 12 months of utility bills for a monthly cross-check. (3) Control stores: a chain has many similar outlets, and staggered roll-outs leave some untreated for a while. That makes a chain the best possible lab.

Unit of evaluation: one site-month savings claim.

  1. Backtest ("placebo in time"). On sites with ≥8 weeks of data before any action: fit the model on weeks 1–4 and predict weeks 5–8. True savings are zero, so any "savings" is model error. Compute NMBE (bias) and CV(RMSE) (noise) on daily totals. Pass: |NMBE| ≤ 5% and CV(RMSE) ≤ 20%. These are my starting thresholds, set between ASHRAE Guideline 14's monthly (±5% / 15%) and hourly (±10% / 30%) limits. Output a minimum claimable saving per site, roughly 2× its backtest error. Anything below that is reported as "within noise", not as a saving.
  2. Placebo stores ("placebo in space"). Run the full pipeline on untreated stores. The claimed savings should centre on 0. Metric: false-claim rate = share of untreated store-months where we'd claim >5% savings. Pass: ≤5%.
  3. Injected savings. Take untreated data, subtract a known change (e.g. 1.5 kW from 23:00–06:00 to mimic an overnight fix, or a flat 3%, 5%, 10%, 20%), run the pipeline, and compare. Pass: estimate within ±20% of the injected value for injections ≥ the minimum claimable saving. This produces a detection curve: what size of saving we can honestly prove.
  4. Bill reconciliation gate. Monthly meter kWh vs the main-meter utility bill. A gap >3% blocks the report and opens a B ticket. The ₹ conversion uses the site's real tariff (kWh vs kVAh, fixed charges, PF penalty).
  5. Non-routine events. Change-point detection on the model's residuals flags step changes that aren't in the action log. Every flag must be labelled (our action / store change / data issue) before the report can be sent.
  6. Number check. Every number in the drafted text must exactly match the computed table. 100% pass, or the report doesn't send.

Golden set: about 30 site-months (past reports with known or disputed outcomes, plus injected cases), re-run every time the method changes, like regression tests in software. After launch: customer disputes per report (target 0), the placebo false-claim rate re-run monthly, the share of reports blocked by data gates, and whether savings persist or decay after month 3. What changes in the report: "18% ± 4%, method X, 3 days excluded (store closed)" instead of a bare "25%". I think the range is a selling point at renewal, not a weakness.

One real trade-off

I'd spend the first ~6 weeks on trusted data (A + B) instead of automating the savings report or building a predictive-maintenance demo. Who this disappoints: the person who builds the monthly reports by hand keeps doing it for another 6 weeks. It also disappoints you and sales, because "trusted data" doesn't demo like "AI predicts failures". My reason is that automating a report built on unchecked data just produces wrong reports faster. To soften it, D's backtest starts in week 3, so the report owner sees progress early.

What would change my mind

  • A big renewal is due within 60 days → D jumps to first, done semi-manually for that customer.
  • The week-1 audit shows mapping errors are rare (<2% of channels) → A shrinks to a checklist, and that time moves to C.
  • A large new contract means installs are about to 5× → E comes back, because kit and install throughput becomes the bottleneck.
  • Customers already ignore alerts (low read/act rates) → redesign C before sending more of anything.
  • Chains will share daily sales or footfall → D becomes much more accurate, and cheaper to do earlier.

An urgent interruption

I'd keep ~20% of capacity unplanned for this. Then I ask three questions. (1) Does it touch trust in our numbers? Example: a chain's ops head says "your dashboard says 18% saved but our bill went up". Then it's top priority, because it's the core promise and it becomes a golden-set case for D. (2) Is there real money with a deadline? Example: you need a pilot proposal or a predictive-maintenance demo by Friday. Then I timebox it (e.g. 2 days, one person) from the buffer, using real data and honest caveats. (3) What slips if I do it? I'd tell you plainly, e.g. "this moves A's switch test by 4 days", and let you make the call. I won't absorb it silently and miss day 30.
